## Today's Learning Goals

By the end, students can present a clear and responsible cybersecurity project.

- **Explain a problem** — Describe one real cyber risk in simple language.
- **Recommend safer actions** — Choose practical controls that reduce the risk.
- **Present clearly** — Use visuals, examples, and a short spoken explanation.
- **Practice teamwork** — Share tasks and give respectful feedback.
- **Stay ethical** — Avoid harmful details and focus on protection.
- **Reflect** — Explain what you learned and what you would improve.

## What Makes a Strong Capstone?

A good project is useful, understandable, safe, and realistic.

- It solves a real problem students, families, schools, or small businesses may face.
- It explains risks in everyday language without teaching harmful techniques.
- It recommends practical safety steps that people can actually follow.
- It includes a visual: poster, diagram, checklist, slide, or mini report.
- It respects privacy, permission, and ethics.

**Capstone formula:** Problem + Risk + Controls + Clear message

## Capstone Project Options

Students may choose one of these safe, high-school-friendly project ideas.

1. **Personal Cyber Safety Toolkit** — Checklist and guide for protecting accounts, phones, and laptops.
2. **Phishing Awareness Campaign** — Poster or slides showing warning signs and safe actions.
3. **Secure My School Network** — Simple plan for safer Wi-Fi, accounts, and student data.
4. **Mini SOC Investigation** — Analyze safe sample logs and write an incident report.
5. **Cybersecurity Career Roadmap** — Learning path for a cybersecurity career that interests you.
6. **Family Safety Guide** — Simple advice for passwords, scams, privacy, and backups.

## Presentation Structure

A 5–7 minute presentation should be clear and easy to follow.

1. **Problem** — What cyber risk are you explaining?
2. **Who is affected** — students, families, schools, or businesses?
3. **Why it matters** — privacy, schoolwork, money, safety, trust.
4. **Recommended controls** — what actions reduce the risk?
5. **Lessons learned** — what should classmates remember?

**Required visual:** Include one poster, diagram, slide, table, checklist, or short incident timeline.

## Simple Scoring Rubric

How the capstone can be evaluated fairly.

| Criteria | Points | Description |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Problem clarity | 20 pts | The risk is explained simply and accurately. |
| Practical controls | 25 pts | Recommendations are safe, realistic, and useful. |
| Ethics and safety | 20 pts | No harmful steps; privacy and permission are respected. |
| Presentation quality | 20 pts | Clear visuals and organized speaking. |
| Reflection | 15 pts | Student explains learning and improvement. |

## Challenge Day Flow

A simple structure for the final class session.

1. **Setup** — Open files, test slides, prepare notes.
2. **Present** — Each student or team presents for 5–7 minutes.
3. **Questions** — Class asks respectful questions.
4. **Feedback** — Give one strength and one improvement idea.
5. **Reflect** — Write final learning reflection.

**Feedback rule:** Be specific, respectful, and focused on learning.

**Good feedback example:** "Your phishing warning signs were clear. You could add one example of a safe reporting step."

## Safe Project Boundaries

The capstone should teach protection, not harmful behavior.

**Allowed:** Use fake examples, safe sample logs, diagrams, checklists, awareness posters, career roadmaps, and defensive recommendations.

**Not allowed:** Do not attack websites, scan school networks, collect real passwords, expose private data, or test accounts without permission.

**Use this safety test:**

- Does the project protect people?
- Does it avoid real private data?
- Does it avoid instructions that could harm systems?
- Would a teacher or parent understand why it is safe?

## Final Reflection

Students connect the course to real life. Answer these questions after your presentation:

- What is the most important cybersecurity idea you learned?
- What digital safety habit will you personally improve?
- Which project or lecture helped you understand cybersecurity most?
- What cybersecurity career or topic would you like to explore next?
- How can students help make their school or family safer online?

*Cybersecurity is a lifelong skill: careful thinking, safe habits, and ethical choices.*

## Course Recap: What You Can Do Now

Students have built a foundation for safer digital life.

- Explain the CIA Triad: confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
- Recognize phishing, malware, weak passwords, and social engineering.
- Use stronger passphrases, MFA, updates, backups, and privacy settings.
- Understand logs, monitoring, SOC, SIEM, and incident response basics.
- Think about rules, risk, ethics, privacy, and cybersecurity careers.
- Present a simple project that helps others stay safer online.
